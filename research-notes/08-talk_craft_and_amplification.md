Scope note: This file is a practitioner knowledge base intended to feed prompts, rubrics and checklists. Every numeric benchmark and criterion is attributed inline. Where a widely repeated claim could not be verified against a fetched source, it is listed under Gaps rather than stated as fact. ## Key Question 1: Idea and framing (what is an "idea worth spreading", throughline, topic vs idea, curator criteria, one-sentence format, "big enough" tests, coach methods)

### Takeaway
TED's own definition is operational: an idea is "more than a story or a list of facts; it draws a larger conclusion from evidence or observations," and must be either new/surprising or a familiar idea with a compelling new argument. Chris Anderson's throughline test (state it in no more than 15 words; ask honestly "who does this idea benefit?") plus organizer-stated criteria (novelty, argument/antagonist, changes perception, local-but-relatable, evidence-based, not motivational, not personal narrative alone) give the app a complete, attributable scorecard for idea quality.

### Cited Findings

**TED's definition and criteria for an idea**
- A TEDx event "showcases well-formed local ideas. An idea is more than a story or a list of facts; it draws a larger conclusion from evidence or observations." A great idea "is either new and surprising, or a familiar idea presented with a compelling new argument that challenges beliefs." — [TED Organizer Guide: What is a TEDx Talk?](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/what-is-a-tedx-talk)
- TED's "idea worth spreading" criteria list: (1) originates in your community but is widely relatable; (2) is defended by an argument, is not self-evident, and may have an antagonist; (3) is novel — "the TED world should not have heard it before, and it should not copy a TED talk"; (4) changes perceptions; (5) can be tested against the TEDx Content Guidelines and Fact-Checking Guide. — [TED Organizer Guide: What is a TEDx Talk?](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/what-is-a-tedx-talk)
- Curators are told: "You're actually looking for a person's idea or innovation, not a person." If you find yourself describing the speaker's identity more than their idea, keep looking for the idea. — [TED Organizer Guide: Select your speakers](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/select-your-speakers-performers)
- Speaker checklist for curators: a local voice few have heard; can "present their field in a new light"; offers a perspective the global TED community may not access; adds diversity (demographic, background, or topic). — [TED Organizer Guide: Select your speakers](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/select-your-speakers-performers)
- TED's four TEDx principles include "Ideas, not profiles": selection depends on "the idea a speaker is actively working on, not their story or credentials." Also: "Personal narrative alone isn't a sufficient basis for selection." — [TED: Speaking at TEDx](https://www.ted.com/tedx/speaking-at-tedx)
- TED's idea-discovery prompts for speakers: "What's a controversy in your field that a general audience would understand?"; "What's a common misconception you'd love to clear up?"; "Why is this idea important, and to whom?"; "Who would disagree with you, and why?"; "How did you carry out this idea in your own work?" — [TED Organizer Guide: Prepare your speaker](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker)
- Official TEDx Speaker Guide: a good idea "can be new or surprising, challenge an existing belief, or offer a new argument behind a familiar idea." "You need to be an expert, though not the world's foremost one." Test procedure: "Write your idea in one or two sentences, then check that it is new, interesting to a varied audience, and factual and realistic. If not, refine it and get feedback from a non-specialist and your organizing team." — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)
- Talk types TED recognizes: the big idea (one or two very strong points); the small idea (engaging take on an interesting topic, not world-changing); the "awe & wonder" talk; the artist's statement; the "issue" talk; the performance. — [TED Organizer Guide: What is a TEDx Talk?](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/what-is-a-tedx-talk)

**Chris Anderson's throughline and tests**
- Throughline = "a core theme that connects all parts of the talk, like a rope to hang each element on." Capture it in no more than 15 words; example: "More choice actually makes us less happy." Find the throughline first, then build the talk around it. — [Readingraphics summary of Anderson, TED Talks](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-ted-talks/)
- Anderson's honesty test: "Ask yourself the question, 'Who does this idea benefit?' ... If the idea only serves you or organization, then I'm sorry to say, it's probably not worth sharing." — [ProRhetoric, quoting Anderson](https://prorhetoric.com/ted-talk-advice-from-the-horses-mouth/)
- Anderson (HBR): presentations succeed or fail on "the quality of the idea, the narrative, and the passion of the speaker"; coaching can fix delivery problems but "there's no way to 'coach in' the basic story." — [HBR: How to Give a Killer Presentation](https://hbr.org/2013/06/how-to-give-a-killer-presentation)
- Anderson's TED talk description promises "four ways to make it work for you" (the four guidelines) but the fetched page did not expose the transcript. — [TED: Chris Anderson, TED's secret to great public speaking](https://www.ted.com/talks/chris_anderson_ted_s_secret_to_great_public_speaking)

**TED curator June Cohen's "new idea" test**
- First test: does the talk offer something new — a new topic or a fresh angle on an old one (e.g., a materials scientist or iceberg photographer approaching climate change differently from Al Gore's talk four years earlier). Talks spread when they create "contagious emotion" (laughter, surprise, being moved, an "aha" moment): "People want to share something when it is emotional." — [June Cohen, What makes a great TED talk (transcript)](https://amara.org/subtitles/0OTt05vRqUYh/en/4/download/TEDxTEDGlobal%20%20June%20Cohen%20%20What%20makes%20a%20great%20TED%20Talk.en.srt)

**Topic vs. issue vs. story vs. idea (coach framework)**
- Brian Miller's four-way distinction: Topic = a subject area ("The science of balance"); Issue = a problem ("The leading cause of death in people over 65 is falling"); Story = a narrative that moves but is not an idea; Idea = "proposes a solution that moves people to action." Method: take your topic/issue/story and look for the idea within it (example idea: "If you don't want the last years of your life to be the worst years of your life" you should focus on balance now). Tests: the wrong question is "How do I get more people to know about my business?"; the right one is "Is my idea TED worthy?"; if the answer to "What's your idea?" is a topic, it isn't yet an idea. — [Mark J. Carter interview with Brian Miller](https://www.markjcarter.com/2025/01/how-to-craft-a-ted-talk-worthy-idea/)
- TEDx organizer Peter Scott: "Pitch an idea, not a topic." Broad advice like "be your authentic self" is too vague. His three selection questions: "Are these people ready? Are these people coachable? Do we have an idea here?" — [Toastmasters Magazine, The Big Red Dot](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2024/may/the-big-red-dot)
- TEDxJacksonville application: "Ninety percent of applicants are rejected based solely on their answer to question #1" (the single unifying idea). "TEDx talks are NOT motivational speeches." "If the core idea of your talk is something like 'be your authentic self' or 'follow your passion,' it will be rejected." "If you want to apply with a personal story, you must draw an idea or clear lesson from that story." "You must have a unique take/idea/opinion about your subject." — [TEDxJacksonville 2024 Speaker Application](https://tedxjacksonville.com/wp-content/uploads/2024-TEDxJacksonville-Speaker-application.pdf)
- TEDxCamden curator Pariti Sutaria: strong ideas "are new and innovative, and they challenge the audience to think, live, or act differently"; talks that work best "are not motivational speeches, hero's journey stories, or research lectures"; talks should still be valuable "five or ten years later"; "the idea is your ... credibility." — [Reed Smith: TEDx insights with Pariti Sutaria](https://www.reedsmith.com/de/perspectives/2023/09/tedx-insights-the-art-of-crafting-evocative-talks-with-pariti-sutaria)

**One-sentence idea formats and coach methods**
- TEDxLinz "The ONE": "Identify one idea, sentence, or question the audience would repeat to friends." Their curation criteria (verbatim capitalization): "NOVELTY of the IDEA", "VALUES behind the proposal", "MOTIVATION", "Potential IMPACT on our Community - local and global", "DIVERSITY - both topic range and representations." Preparation aspects: personal story, values, key questions (incl. rhetorical), "short bold statements." — [TEDxLinz speaker document](https://www.tedxlinz.at/_files/ugd/c88ea0_56ec8c5e1ea24496879bfe6ef88b919d.pdf)
- Ryan Foland (4x TEDx speaker, lead TEDx speaker trainer): condense the message into one sentence, read it aloud, refine until it captures the core idea; use it as the anchor and check every story/example supports it; list 3–5 supporting points and cut any that don't strengthen the takeaway; test by asking a small trusted group to summarize the takeaway in their own words — if it doesn't match, adjust. "Just one." — [Ryan Foland Substack](https://ryanfoland.substack.com/p/from-overloaded-to-on-point-why-a)
- Devin Marks (TED coach) topics on record: the breakthrough idea as "the one sentence that will make us stand up and pay attention"; six elements of a "sticky story"; "texture" (language appealing to all five senses); the rule of three; "winnable wins" (ask the audience for a small achievable first step). — [Julie Masters podcast page with Devin Marks](https://juliemasters.com/devin-marks-decoding-the-breakaway-ted-talk-how-to-create-an-idea-worth-sharing/)
- Taylor Conroy (Thought-Leader): "You have a message inside you"; pick a niche early (examples: social good, trauma, addiction, parenting, climate change); you don't need to be an expert (a Southwest flight attendant's TEDx led to $75,000+ in later earnings per Conroy). No step-by-step idea method is published on the page. — [EOFire interview with Taylor Conroy](https://www.eofire.com/podcast/taylorconroy2/)
- Tricia Brouk (exec producer, TEDxLincolnSquare, "hundreds of applications"): "Focus on the idea, not your business" — TEDx prioritizes "ideas that the audience can engage with and adopt"; align with the event theme; contribute to the community (be explicit about how the idea could affect the audience and wider community). — [Tricia Brouk: How to Apply to TEDx Events](https://triciabrouk.com/how-to-apply-to-tedx-events/)
- Toastmasters' TEDx organizer: "It's ideas first. Everything else comes after." Rejected proposals are "ordinary, unoriginal, or half-baked, or come from someone without enthusiasm for the idea." — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk)
- The Speaker Lab: choose a new idea or a fresh take; professional speakers should pitch something distinct from their usual keynote; "focus on one idea, not a list of three points or five steps"; speak from your domain of authority; tie your niche to a broader shared need. — [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker/)
- Entrepreneur (TEDx speaker): check whether the topic is already saturated (e.g., "pushing through rejection" has been covered many times) and be ready to explain how yours differs; organizers will ask if you advance. — [Entrepreneur: Here's How to Apply to Give a TEDx Talk](https://www.entrepreneur.com/leadership/heres-how-to-apply-to-give-a-tedx-talk/422943)
- TED Commandment #2: "Thou Shalt Dream a Great Dream, or Show Forth a Wondrous New Thing, Or Share Something Thou Hast Never Shared Before." #1: "Thou Shalt Not Simply Trot Out thy Usual Shtick." — [TED Commandments (Conor Neill transcription)](https://conorneill.com/2010/06/24/the-ted-commandments-10-rules-every-speaker-needs-to-know/)

### Inferences
- A usable "idea test battery" for the app, each item attributable: (a) ≤15-word throughline (Anderson); (b) "Who does this idea benefit?" honesty test (Anderson); (c) is it an idea, not a topic/issue/story (Miller; Scott; TED); (d) novelty: would the TED world have heard it? is it a copy of an existing TED talk? (TED); (e) arguable: who would disagree and why? (TED discovery prompts); (f) evidence: does it draw a larger conclusion from evidence/observations? (TED); (g) relatable beyond the local community (TED); (h) changes perception / makes audience think, live or act differently (TED; Sutaria); (i) durable in 5–10 years (Sutaria); (j) not a motivational platitude ("be authentic", "follow your passion") (TEDxJacksonville); (k) not personal narrative alone (TED).
- A one-sentence idea template consistent with the sources: "[Specific claim that challenges a belief] because [evidence/mechanism], which means [what the audience should see/do differently]." Miller's example fits a "warning + action now" pattern.

### Gaps
- Anderson's four guidelines from his TED talk and the named lists in his book (four talk styles to avoid, named openers/closers) could not be verified from fetched pages; summaries only confirm counts ("4 ways to start strong and 7 ways to end powerfully").
- Devin Marks's "six elements of a sticky story" and Donovan's topic-selection criteria are behind paywalls/podcast audio and were not verifiable.
- Taylor Conroy publishes no step-by-step ideation method on public pages fetched; his content is primarily marketing claims.

## Key Question 2: Application and pitch (form fields, how to answer, what gets selected/rejected, theme personalization, follow-up, pitch video, pay-to-play ethics)

### Takeaway
There is no central TED application; each event runs its own form, but the fields converge: a one-paragraph idea statement (often 100–250 words), a 1–2 sentence distillation, a 60-second to 3-minute pitch video, "why you," theme/local connection, a short bio (≈250 words), and coachability commitments (3–5 hours/week; 60+ hours total). Organizers report rejecting most applicants on the idea statement alone, reward theme alignment and non-commercial framing, and TED's rules make any payment to speak disqualifying.

### Cited Findings

**How applications work (TED-level)**
- "There is no central application to speak at a TEDx event through TED." "TED doesn't accept speaker applications for TEDx events." Each event picks its own speakers; submit only if the team has posted a public application form. "Submitting multiple nominations for the same person does not increase the chance of selection." — [TED: Speaking at TEDx](https://www.ted.com/tedx/speaking-at-tedx)
- Sponsor representatives and event volunteers cannot speak at events they support; duplicate talks won't be published; TEDx isn't meant for repeat speakers. — [TED: Speaking at TEDx](https://www.ted.com/tedx/speaking-at-tedx)
- Curators are told to confirm the speaker "is not a repeat, circuit, or commercial speaker," including those with multiple TEDx talks already published or seeking several TEDx events in a short period; red flags: advertising/selling a business, political or religious agendas, bad science, polarizing "us vs. them" topics, copying an admired TED talk. — [TED Organizer Guide: Select your speakers](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/select-your-speakers-performers)
- Vetting organizers are told to do: verify credentials via university sites and journals; ask an unaffiliated professor or a journalist to fact-check; for science/health claims, investigate major criticisms. — [TED Organizer Guide: Select your speakers](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/select-your-speakers-performers)

**Actual form fields (verbatim where available)**
- TEDxJacksonville 2024: Q1 "What single, unifying IDEA is at the heart of your TEDx talk? Be clear and specific." + proposed length (options: 4 minutes or less; 5 to 9 minutes; 10 to 12 minutes). Q2 "Distill your idea worth sharing into 1 or 2 sentences." Q3 "Add a link to a 1-minute video of you outlining your idea and why it is important that it be shared now." Q4 "Why are you the right person to speak about this idea?" Q5 recent accomplishment (100 words or less). Q6 connection to Jacksonville. Each answer "100–250 words." Optional: brief bio, links to prior presentation videos, resume/portfolio, photo. Agreement checkboxes: willing to work with a speaker coach; "willing to spend 3 to 5 hours per week preparing"; attend required rehearsals; coaching is a condition of acceptance regardless of experience. — [TEDxJacksonville 2024 Speaker Application](https://tedxjacksonville.com/wp-content/uploads/2024-TEDxJacksonville-Speaker-application.pdf)
- TEDxMinneapolis: 60–90 second video covering "your idea, why it matters, and why you are the best person to share it." Idea criteria: "New, interesting, evidence-based and realistic ... and shareable within 18 minutes. They aim for 10-14 minutes." Speaker criteria: lives/works in Minnesota; can attend two "Speaker Checkpoint" coaching sessions; "receptive to coaching and feedback"; the best person in the Twin Cities to speak on the topic "with peer credibility"; "compelling, authentic, and engaging." Speakers selected 4–6 months before the event; assigned coach; meet at least monthly. — [TEDxMinneapolis: Submit an Idea](https://www.tedxminneapolis.com/submit-an-idea/)
- TEDxLinz: pitch recording "max 3-minutes-long" with name/contact plus answers to two questions: why you're the right person, and what's controversial about the idea. — [TEDxLinz speaker document](https://www.tedxlinz.at/_files/ugd/c88ea0_56ec8c5e1ea24496879bfe6ef88b919d.pdf)
- TEDxDirigo: looks for "Local voices", "A novel invention, design or vision", "A fresh philosophical argument", "New historical perspectives", "Strange and unusual discoveries." Does not accept: sales pitches or fundraising asks; recycled talks; last-minute talks; anything through a "pay-to-play" arrangement. "Not enough to get selected": repeated nominations or requests; mentioning past TED/TEDx talks; promoting yourself as a keynote or motivational speaker; taking courses or coaching aimed at landing a TEDx talk. Commitments: 250-word bio and headshot; Speaker Launch event; at least four hours of 1:1 coaching; full draft at Speaker Camp; dress rehearsals with no notes or podium; "60+ hours" total preparation; most talks 6–12 minutes. — [TEDxDirigo: Speak](https://www.tedxdirigo.com/speak)
- TEDxAlabaster (600+ applicants for 2026; 8–12 minute talks; prep 3–4 months): an accepted applicant credits tailoring the pitch to the theme "Ripples of Revelation" using the event's own language; ask "why would a room of strangers care?"; for the video, "The organizer has to envision you on the stage" — well-lit, dressed as if on stage, clear and confident. — [Speaker Accelerator: Tips for Applying to TEDxAlabaster](https://speakeraccelerator.substack.com/p/tips-for-applying-to-tedxalabaster)
- TEDxCamden: 300+ applications, eight selected; two selection rounds (approx. May–August); curation September–January; first two months after selection are heavy feedback/revisions; talks 5–15 min, usually about 10. — [Reed Smith: Pariti Sutaria](https://www.reedsmith.com/de/perspectives/2023/09/tedx-insights-the-art-of-crafting-evocative-talks-with-pariti-sutaria)
- Peter Scott's team (Toastmasters): forms require a sample of your script and an explanation of why you're the right person; auditions are in person (video auditions rarely accepted); you read the script, then are asked to summarize your core message, how you'll support it outside TEDx, and how you feel about coaching. "Looking at the context of what wins, it's anecdotes, examples, and stories with an underlying theme." Ask curators for feedback whether or not selected. — [Toastmasters: The Big Red Dot](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2024/may/the-big-red-dot)
- Toastmasters organizer: state in the proposal "that you understand the TED platform and are willing to work with a coaching team and accept feedback" — raises your chances. Research events at ted.com/tedx/events: how long the event has run, attendee cap, theme, venue/stage design, production quality, past speakers and video quality. — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk)

**Pitch length, structure, odds, timing, follow-up (coach/aggregator guidance)**
- The Speaker Lab: keep the written pitch to about 200 words, structured as 1 sentence on the idea, 3 on your expertise, 2 on why this audience needs it now; avoid generic motivational language; "lead with what makes the idea unconventional or surprising, then show its relevance to the local community and the wider world." Odds: "Most events accept 5 to 10% of applicants"; "Most speakers apply to 5 to 10 events before landing 1 to 2 acceptances per year"; applying to multiple events raises acceptance closer to 20%. Target events 6–8 months out; organizers start working with you 3–4 months before; most speakers are given 3, 6, or 9 minutes; if rejected, ask the decision-maker to suggest another event. — [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker/)
- Entrepreneur (TEDx speaker): apply 6–8 months before; selection often takes 2–4 rounds; "Apply broadly" (hometowns, schools, places lived, easy-travel areas); research 5–10 events; some ask for a 1–2 minute video of your main idea — use it to test whether you have a clear thesis, a strong hook, and enough material for a 12–18 minute talk; expect 3–6 months of consistent submissions; share the idea with a former TEDx speaker/organizer/coach beforehand. — [Entrepreneur: Here's How to Apply](https://www.entrepreneur.com/leadership/heres-how-to-apply-to-give-a-tedx-talk/422943)
- Patrick Frank guide: pitch should "explain why your idea matters now and what sets it apart"; "'What if stress isn't the enemy?' is a stronger opener than simply saying you'll talk about workplace stress"; video where requested 2–3 minutes showing speaking style and concept; follow up with a brief friendly email if no reply after two weeks; ask for feedback; track applications in a spreadsheet; organizers choose 6–8 months before the event; some speakers submitted up to 86 applications before selection (citing Thought-Leader). — [Patrick Frank: How to Become a TEDx Speaker](https://patrickfrank.beehiiv.com/p/1-how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker-a-step-by-step-guide)
- Tricia Brouk: vet the organizer (TEDx history; dedicated roles such as stage manager, lighting designer, tech director; balanced lineup). — [Tricia Brouk](https://triciabrouk.com/how-to-apply-to-tedx-events/)

**Pay-to-play ethics**
- "Speakers never pay, directly or indirectly." Services that charge fees to get you a TEDx talk "are not legitimate." Report paid offers to tedx@ted.com. "Talks tied to financial transactions are ineligible for publication." Coaching, participation and attendance are free for speakers ("Forever free"). — [TED: Speaking at TEDx](https://www.ted.com/tedx/speaking-at-tedx)
- "TEDx events may neither pay nor charge speakers." "Sponsors of your event cannot be speakers and can never present from the stage." "Organizers cannot be speakers at events to which they contribute." — [TEDx Rules](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/before-you-start/tedx-rules)
- Enforcement example: TED cancelled a Dubai TEDx event and revoked the licence (Oct 2025) after reporting showed speakers were asked to pay up to $25,000 (one quoted $15,000 for a "premium slot"); organizers admitted a "serious mistake." — [Khaleej Times, 7 Oct 2025](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/uae/dubai-tedx-event-cancelled-alleged-25000-speaker-fees)
- Speakers are unpaid and travel may not be reimbursed; many events therefore prefer local speakers. — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk); [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker/)
- Note the ethical line: paying a coach to improve your talk is not prohibited, but TEDxDirigo explicitly says "taking courses or coaching aimed at landing a TEDx talk" is "not enough to get selected" and pay-to-play arrangements are rejected. — [TEDxDirigo](https://www.tedxdirigo.com/speak)

### Inferences
- A field-by-field answer guide the app can enforce: (1) Idea statement, 100–250 words, leading with the surprising claim, then evidence, then why now, then local/global relevance (Jacksonville limits; Speaker Lab structure; Frank's "what if" opener). (2) One/two-sentence distillation ≤15 words (Anderson) framed as a claim, not a topic. (3) "Why you": domain authority + peer credibility + original work on the idea, not résumé prestige (TEDxMinneapolis; TED "ideas not profiles"). (4) "Why now": tie to an event theme sentence and a timely trend/controversy (Alabaster; TED discovery prompts). (5) Video: 60–180 seconds depending on event, record standing, well-lit, as if on stage, stating idea, why it matters, why you (Minneapolis; Alabaster). (6) Bio: ≈250 words for forms, 4–7 sentences for YouTube (Dirigo; TED get-views page). (7) Explicit coachability statement and availability (Toastmasters; Jacksonville).
- Reasonable acceptance-rate planning numbers: 5–10% per event; 300–600+ applicants per mid-size event; apply to 5–10 events 6–8 months out. These are coach/organizer-reported, not TED statistics.

### Gaps
- No official TED data on application volumes or acceptance rates; figures are from individual events and The Speaker Lab.
- TEDxSydney, TEDxToronto, TEDxBoston, TEDxGateway and TEDxBangalore application guides were not retrieved (TEDxSydney fetch returned only a homepage; search budget then expired).
- Title formulas specific to applications were not found; TED's title guidance (Section 6) applies to YouTube titles and can be reused.

## Key Question 3: Talk structure and writing (structure models, hooks, story, evidence, endings/CTA, word-count/WPM benchmarks, what made top talks popular, writing failure modes)

### Takeaway
TED's official structure is four beats (make them care → explain the idea → evidence and implementation → how the idea affects the audience), with "invisible" structure, present-tense conversational prose and aggressive cutting; Anderson adds the throughline and five tools, Duarte the "what is / what could be" sparkline ending in a "new bliss", and Gallo the emotional/novel/memorable triad with a three-message map. Pace data puts popular TED speakers at ~154–201 wpm (avg ~173) against a recommended presentation range of 120–160 wpm, so an 18-minute script lands roughly 2,200–2,900 words and a 10-minute TEDx talk roughly 1,300–1,600 words.

### Cited Findings

**TED's official structure and writing guidance**
- TEDx Speaker Guide suggested structure: "1. Make the audience care with a relatable example or intriguing idea. 2. Explain your idea clearly and with conviction. 3. Present your evidence and how the idea could be implemented. 4. Close by describing how the idea could affect the audience if they accepted it." "The structure should be invisible. Don't announce what you're about to do." "The main goal is to communicate an idea. Storytelling and emotion are tools, not ends in themselves." — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)
- Introduction: "Hook the audience with something they care about, state your idea quickly, and don't focus too much on yourself." Body: "List all evidence, order it by what the audience needs to know first and by how exciting it is, then cut aggressively. Spend more time on new information, favor empirical over anecdotal evidence, limit jargon, and respectfully address controversies and counterarguments. Keep citations out of the main flow." Conclusion: "End positively, explaining how the idea could affect the audience's lives. Don't simply summarize, and avoid pitches such as fundraising, book covers, or corporate logos. A call to action is fine if appropriate." Script: "Be concise and natural, use present tense, and choose strong, interesting verbs." — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)
- Organizer checklist for a script: Structure (clear introduction, middle, end); Brevity (fits the slot; "identify the single major point"); Purpose (every point supports the main idea and shows why it matters). Language should reflect "how the speaker actually talks (not how they write)." Use personal anecdotes "sparingly, and only ones that clearly support the idea." — [TED Organizer Guide: Outline + script](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/outline-script)
- Talk length norms: 18 minutes is traditional; "Some of the most impactful talks have been about 5 minutes, and 5 to 8 minutes is common for concise ideas"; interviews/dialogues up to 30 minutes. — [TED Organizer Guide: What is a TEDx Talk?](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/what-is-a-tedx-talk)
- TEDx Rules: "TEDx talks are typically under 18 minutes"; talks of 3 to 9 minutes are described as ideal; talks over 18 minutes are reviewed before publication; "No panels or audience Q&A are permitted." — [TEDx Rules](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/before-you-start/tedx-rules)
- TED to organizers: "Continue to shorten your stage talks (we find that 8-10 minutes is a sweet spot)." — [TED Organizer Guide: Promote your talks](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/branding-promotions/promote-your-talks)
- Peter Scott: "If you can't find that value in 12 minutes, you're not likely to do a good job in 18." Talks should center on one major idea "with no more than three subtopics." "We get the words right before we learn how to say them." A "script lockdown" date after which no changes are allowed and fact-checking begins. — [Toastmasters: The Big Red Dot](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2024/may/the-big-red-dot)

**Chris Anderson (HBR / TED Talks book)**
- "When I think about compelling presentations, I think about taking an audience on a journey." Choosing start and end points is the hardest step; judge what the audience already knows; explain in the intro why the topic matters; don't overload ("Too many points leave no time for the key ones and make the talk abstract"); use concrete examples and compare unfamiliar ideas to familiar things; cover a few topics in depth; "Present a problem first, then develop the solution as a story." — [TED Blog excerpt](https://blog.ted.com/chris-anderson-shares-his-tips-for-giving-a-killer-presentation/); [Gigazine summary of HBR article](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20130613-how-to-make-killer-presentation)
- Five tools for building an idea: Connection (trust/bond early: eye contact, vulnerability, laughter); Narration (stories); Explanation (start with what the audience knows and build in layers); Persuasion (dismantle wrong beliefs first; priming and reasoning); Revelation (show the idea: "wonder walk, demo, and dreamscape"). Memorize your opening and closing lines even if you don't script. — [Readingraphics summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-ted-talks/)
- Two named presentation-ruining pitfalls from the CFA digest of the HBR article: taking too long to make a point, and too much technical jargon. — [CFA Institute digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2013/11/how-to-give-a-killer-presentation-digest-summary)

**Nancy Duarte's sparkline**
- Beginning: establish "what is" (status quo), then contrast with "what could be"; widen the gap (compared to a movie's inciting incident). Middle: alternate between "what is" and "what could be," like sailing against the wind — "you tack back and forth, using their resistance to move your idea forward." End: a call to action "placed at the very end," then describe the world with the idea adopted as a "new bliss," poetically and dramatically ("This is utopia with my idea adopted"). — [Science That Sticks summary of Duarte's talk](https://2023sciencethatsticks.substack.com/p/the-secret-structure-of-great-talks)
- Duarte prep: plan "two natural ending points" — if running long, stop at the first; the audience misses the inspirational close but still hears the most important information. Have a coach make sure you explain *why* ideas matter ("the 'why' behind ideas makes them spread more than the 'how'"). "Murder your darlings." — [Duarte: 11 ways to prepare](https://www.duarte.com/10-ways-to-prepare-for-a-ted-format-talk/)

**Carmine Gallo, Talk Like TED (9 secrets)**
- Grouped as Emotional (1 Passion; 2 Storytelling — three story types: personal, about others, brand success; 3 Have a Conversation — rate, volume, pitch, pauses), Novel (4 Teach something new; 5 Deliver jaw-dropping moments — Gates's mosquito moment was under 5% of his 18 minutes; 6 Lighten up), Memorable (7 The 18-minute rule, justified by "cognitive backlog"; 8 Multisensory mental pictures — photos over words, props; 9 Stay in your lane/authenticity). Message map: a Twitter-friendly headline, three key messages, and supporting stories/stats/examples (rule of three). — [Carmine Gallo: Cracking the TED Code](https://www.carminegallo.com/cracking-the-ted-code-takeaways-from-talk-like-ted-the-9-public-speaking-secrets-of-the-worlds-top-minds/)

- Devin Marks "winnable wins": ask for a small achievable first step rather than a big change. — [Julie Masters / Devin Marks](https://juliemasters.com/devin-marks-decoding-the-breakaway-ted-talk-how-to-create-an-idea-worth-sharing/)

**Openings**

**Story, evidence, jargon**

**What made top talks popular (empirical)**
- Science of People (760 raters; 50 TED.com talks from 2010, 15–20 min): top talks averaged 7.36M views and 465 hand gestures per 18 minutes vs 124K views and 272 gestures for bottom talks; high-charisma talks used at least 240 gestures; most popular talks rated 43% higher in charisma; most popular speakers had 30.5% higher vocal variety; speakers who smiled at least 14 seconds were rated higher in intelligence; ratings were the same with sound on or muted; memorized/scripted lines were linked to lower charisma, so "practicing delivery in several different ways" is recommended; ad-libbing and storytelling linked to higher ratings. — [Science of People](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/secrets-of-a-successful-ted-talk/)
- June Cohen: talks spread on contagious emotion and "urgent learning" (viewers pass along what they feel others should know). — [June Cohen transcript](https://amara.org/subtitles/0OTt05vRqUYh/en/4/download/TEDxTEDGlobal%20%20June%20Cohen%20%20What%20makes%20a%20great%20TED%20Talk.en.srt)

**Word count and pace benchmarks**
- Five popular TED talks measured: Tony Robbins 201 wpm; Susan Cain 176; Simon Sinek 170; Ken Robinson 165; Brené Brown 154; average 173 wpm, range 154–201. English for Academic Purposes 30:79–94](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/547664)

**Writing failure modes (TED's own lists)**
- Organizer "quirks to eliminate": talking about the talk; apologizing to the audience; asking questions where statements would be clearer; too much vocabulary; redundancies; repetition; reading quotes; overstating points; utopianism; dystopianism; forced emotions; TED puns; purely anecdotal evidence; failing to admit controversy; unnecessary slides. Also avoid "overstatements, utopianism, fake emotion, fake self-deprecation, TED puns, and suspicious claims." — [TED Organizer Guide: Outline + script](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/outline-script)
- Speaker Guide "things to avoid": talking about how you'll talk; focusing too much on yourself; opening with a string of statistics; too much jargon or excessive terminology; citations interrupting the explanation; ending with a pitch; using the conclusion just to summarize; bullet points/heavy text on slides; images you don't own; serif fonts on slides; forcing emotion on heavy topics. — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)

### Inferences
- Word-count calculator (derived from the cited pace data, not a TED figure): at a conversational-to-TED range of 130–160 wpm, 18 min ≈ 2,340–2,880 words; 12 min ≈ 1,560–1,920; 10 min ≈ 1,300–1,600; 8 min ≈ 1,040–1,280; 6 min ≈ 780–960. Because popular TED speakers average ~173 wpm, a script written at 150 wpm leaves a natural buffer for pauses and laughter. Build in Duarte's two ending points and Scott's "if you can't do it in 12 you can't do it in 18" rule to keep the talk under the slot.
- A composite "TEDx script spine" supported by multiple sources: Hook (single surprising fact, specific moment, or "what if" question; no greeting/meta) → Why care (what is) → Idea stated early, ≤15 words → Evidence/explanation layered from familiar to new, with counterargument acknowledged → Implication (what could be) → Specific, winnable call to action → New-bliss close (no summary, no pitch).

## Key Question 4: Slides and visuals (TEDx slide guidance, when to use none, text/font/aspect rules, video rules, stage setup, common mistakes)

### Takeaway
TED's rule is slides optional and minimal: one idea per slide, 1–2 lines of text at most, sans serif at ≥42 pt (Speaker Guide) or ≥24 pt with ≤3 sizes (Anderson), 16:9 at 1920×1080, edge-to-edge owned images, no bullets, no logos, video clips ≤30 seconds (Anderson) or under one minute (HBR summary), and a blank screen when not referring to slides. The stage is a small red circle/"dot" the speaker stays inside, with a clicker and confidence monitor rehearsed in advance, no podium, and a cue for time running out.

### Cited Findings

**Official TEDx slide guidance**
- "Slides are optional, and many strong TED and TEDx talks have none." Show several slides each with one idea rather than one complex slide; avoid text-heavy and "headline and bullet points" slides; limit text to one or two lines; readable from the farthest seat; simple sans serif font; design for 16:9 (1920×1080 or 1280×720) or 4:3; load onto house computers plus a backup device; rehearse slide changes with the remote or have someone offstage advance slides. — [TED Organizer Guide: Prepare your speaker](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker)
- Speaker Guide: use slides "only if they clarify information rather than distract"; photos running edge-to-edge work well; images help the audience remember people, places, things without verbal description; one point per graph; "Use as little [text] as possible. Avoid bullet points"; confirm resolution/aspect with organizers, else 1920×1080 at 16:9; keep visuals within broadcast-safe zones; "font size of 42 points or larger"; common sans serif (Helvetica, Verdana); send custom fonts ahead; use only images you own or have permission for; cite Creative Commons sources at the bottom of the slide; serif fonts are on the avoid list. — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)
- Speakers must own the rights to all slide images; generative AI tools should not be used with copyrighted material or with input that isn't original to the speaker. — [TED Organizer Guide: Prepare your speaker](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker)
- Video rules affecting slides: talk videos must have no superimposed text, logos, subtitles or watermarks; slides must appear in full frame, not picture-in-picture or split screen; speakers must disclose third-party material beforehand and get written permission; organizers must confirm images, music and clips are cleared for YouTube. — [TEDx Rules](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/before-you-start/tedx-rules)

**Anderson's slide rules (TED Talks book)**
- Visuals only when they (a) reveal something hard to describe in words, (b) explain/enhance, or (c) delight aesthetically. One core idea per slide (short phrase, question, or image); switch to a blank screen when not referring to slides; avoid standard templates; high-resolution images ideally on a black background; one simple legible typeface, no more than three font sizes, at least 24 pt; no italics or underlining (bold is fine); build content progressively; videos only when essential and "under 30 seconds"; no flashy transitions; send slides in advance with a zipped backup (fonts and videos) on USB. — [Readingraphics summary of Anderson](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-ted-talks/)
- HBR summary: many TED speakers used no PowerPoint; artists/architects/photographers/designers can use visuals to show work; keep videos under one minute; avoid promotional videos, videos with music, and videos featuring the presenter; "whatever you do, don't read from PowerPoint slides." — [Gigazine summary of HBR](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20130613-how-to-make-killer-presentation); [HBR](https://hbr.org/2013/06/how-to-give-a-killer-presentation)
- Duarte: print the deck as 9-up handouts (sticky-note size) to rearrange; cutting a 40-minute talk to 18 means cutting at least half the slides. — [Duarte: 11 ways](https://www.duarte.com/10-ways-to-prepare-for-a-ted-format-talk/)
- Gallo secret #8: avoid word-heavy slides, use more photos, use props. — [Carmine Gallo](https://www.carminegallo.com/cracking-the-ted-code-takeaways-from-talk-like-ted-the-9-public-speaking-secrets-of-the-worlds-top-minds/)

**Stage setup**
- "Request as much on-stage dress rehearsal as possible, using the clicker and confidence monitor." Practice "standing planted in one spot. Have a friend stop you from pacing or shifting your weight." — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)
- Dress rehearsal covers where speakers get mic'd, how they step on/off stage, "and how they're cued when time runs out"; run the talk several times onstage with slides and host intro; avoid podiums/lecterns; "stand still while speaking, since pacing disrupts video"; wardrobe: no stripes, complex patterns, bright colors, dangling jewelry. — [TED Organizer Guide: Rehearsals](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/rehearsals)
- "Speakers should stay inside the red circle, though gestures are welcome." Memorization is required "because there are no teleprompters." — [Toastmasters: The Big Red Dot](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2024/may/the-big-red-dot)
- "Plan for the stage, which is usually the small 'TED dot.' Adjust gestures and movement to fit it." Attend the dry run a day or two early to check lights, stage area and monitors. — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk)
- TEDxDirigo dress rehearsals: on stage "with no notes or podium." — [TEDxDirigo](https://www.tedxdirigo.com/speak)
- Host introductions should be about 30 seconds; sessions ≤105 minutes, typically 90 with four full-length talks. — [TED Organizer Guide: Prepare your speaker](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker)

### Inferences
- Slide checklist for the app: (1) Do you need slides at all? (2) ≤1 idea per slide; ≤2 lines; no bullets. (3) Sans serif, ≥42 pt (TEDx guide) — treat Anderson's ≥24 pt as a floor only for large venues; ≤3 font sizes. (4) 16:9, 1920×1080, safe margins. (5) Owned/licensed images, CC credit on slide, no logos, no company branding. (6) Video ≤30 s (Anderson) and never over 60 s (HBR summary), no promo/music/presenter videos. (7) Blank/black slide when not referencing. (8) Backup on USB with fonts and videos; send in advance. (9) Rehearse with clicker and confidence monitor; plan an offstage operator if preferred.
- Common mistakes seen across sources: reading from slides, bullet lists, logos/book covers in the close, uncleared images (which can block YouTube publication), slides that need picture-in-picture (prohibited), and pacing outside the circle (ruins camera framing).

- No official TED page describing the red rug/circle's dimensions or origin was retrieved; evidence of the "red circle/dot" is from Toastmasters and TEDxLinz, not TED.
- Confidence-monitor conventions (what is shown: timer, next slide, notes) were not described by any fetched TED page.

## Key Question 5: Rehearsal, memorization and delivery (evidence-based memorization, rehearsal counts and schedules, delivery metrics, nerves, timing control, blanks)

### Takeaway
TED's standard is "rehearse until reciting doesn't feel like reciting," using record-transcribe-edit-repeat cycles across a 6-month schedule (bi-weekly rehearsals at 2 months out, weekly at 1 month, a deliberate 2-week break, dress rehearsals 1–2 days out); Anderson says the best talks were memorized word-for-word and the danger zone is the half-memorized "valley of awkwardness." Retrieval-practice research (Roediger & Karpicke 2006) justifies rehearsing from memory over re-reading, Brooks (2014) justifies "I am excited" over "calm down," and Science of People's metrics (≥240 gestures/18 min, high vocal variety, ≥14 s smiling, strong first 7 seconds) plus Anderson's 5–6-person eye-contact method and a 120–160 wpm pace give concrete delivery targets.

### Cited Findings

**Official TED/TEDx rehearsal guidance**
- Sample timeline: 6 months out: thesis and basic outline; 5 months: script or detailed outline; 4 months: second draft and first rehearsals; 3 months: final draft and more rehearsals; 2 months: bi-weekly rehearsals; 1 month: weekly rehearsals; 2 weeks: "Take a break. (Don't think about the talk.)"; 1 week: rehearsals; 1–2 days: dress rehearsals. — [TED Organizer Guide: Outline + script](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/outline-script)
- "Start rehearsing as early as possible, and rehearse until the talk no longer sounds scripted." "Record and listen to rehearsals to identify issues." Memorization: "Record, transcribe, and edit the talk, then repeat the process." "If a speaker sounds too rehearsed, they need more rehearsal. Rehearse until reciting doesn't feel like reciting. Alternatively, let go of the script and rely on knowing the idea." Speakers "should rehearse as many times as possible"; use one or two practice partners and small live-audience rehearsals; organizers should ask speakers "to step away from the talk and rehearsing for a week or two to gain perspective." Tone: "Imagine telling the one story that makes a dinner table pause"; honest, contagious emotions ("wonder, optimism, anger, or surprise"). Quirks to remove: whispering, yelling, nervous pacing, awkward stillness, forced emotions. — [TED Organizer Guide: Rehearsals](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/rehearsals)
- Speaker Guide: "Rehearse until you're completely comfortable, with many different audiences, including experts, non-experts, people you love, and people you fear." "Sounding 'over-rehearsed' really means sounding stilted, so aim to speak as if talking to one person in a spontaneous conversation." Timing: "Practice with a visible clock counting down until you hit the right time every time." Delivery: "Take a breath, then deliver the talk the way you practiced." — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)
- Speaker expectations: "Will rehearse their talk for weeks or months in advance"; must attend at least one rehearsal including a dress rehearsal. — [TED Organizer Guide: Prepare your speaker](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker)

**Memorization standards from Anderson, TED staff and speakers**
- "Many of our best and most popular TED Talks have been memorized word for word." Speakers who haven't fully memorized pass through "the 'valley of awkwardness'" that the audience can sense; the way out is "rehearsing enough times that the flow of words becomes second nature." — [TED Blog excerpt of HBR](https://blog.ted.com/chris-anderson-shares-his-tips-for-giving-a-killer-presentation/)
- CFA digest of the same article: don't read from a script or teleprompter; memorize word for word; use nervousness to advantage. — [CFA digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2013/11/how-to-give-a-killer-presentation-digest-summary)
- Anderson's book: rehearse repeatedly until you know the talk well enough to focus on the meaning; memorize opening and closing lines even without a script. — [Readingraphics](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-ted-talks/)
- June Cohen: rehearsal is "the biggest secret" and the gap between a medium and a great talk "is often practice"; Hans Rosling rehearsed for hours with a countdown clock. — [June Cohen transcript](https://amara.org/subtitles/0OTt05vRqUYh/en/4/download/TEDxTEDGlobal%20%20June%20Cohen%20%20What%20makes%20a%20great%20TED%20Talk.en.srt)
- Counter-view: Science of People found memorized lines linked to lower charisma and recommends practicing delivery "in several different ways." — [Science of People](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/secrets-of-a-successful-ted-talk/); Toastmasters' organizer: "Don't memorize the full speech. Know the subject well enough to speak conversationally." — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk)
- Event practice: TEDxCamden speakers "memorize their script and work with a speaker coach"; talks are fact-checked before filming. — [Reed Smith: Pariti Sutaria](https://www.reedsmith.com/de/perspectives/2023/09/tedx-insights-the-art-of-crafting-evocative-talks-with-pariti-sutaria); "TEDx talks are delivered from memory. Plan your stage positions, emphasized words, and gestures. Practice memorizing passages from a book to build the skill." — [Entrepreneur](https://www.entrepreneur.com/leadership/heres-how-to-apply-to-give-a-tedx-talk/422943)

**Rehearsal volume reported by speakers/events**
- Jia Jiang (TEDx → TED.com): 4 TEDx talks, "30+ hours of writing and practice, 50+ hours of live rehearsal." — [Jia Jiang](https://www.rejectiontherapy.com/blog/2017/7/25/how-i-got-a-ted-talk)
- TEDxDirigo: "60+ hours" total preparation, minimum four hours of 1:1 coaching. — [TEDxDirigo](https://www.tedxdirigo.com/speak)
- TEDxJacksonville: 3–5 hours per week of preparation. — [TEDxJacksonville application](https://tedxjacksonville.com/wp-content/uploads/2024-TEDxJacksonville-Speaker-application.pdf)
- Peter Scott: about six months for an 18-minute talk; two formal rehearsals (final-version unveiling, then dress with lighting/mics/wardrobe). — [Toastmasters: The Big Red Dot](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2024/may/the-big-red-dot)
- David Meerman Scott: first run to family, then coach Nick Morgan, then four to five live audiences (a university class, a corporate team, ~200 at National Geographic, two ~75-person sessions, a Toastmasters group), filming several runs; major rewrite one week before the event. — [David Meerman Scott](https://www.davidmeermanscott.com/blog/2013/05/how-to-prepare-and-deliver-a-tedx-talk.html)
- Duarte: solicit feedback from trusted presenters several times (she did it four times before designing slides); rehearse with an honest coach; videotape final practices; "Do one more full-timed rehearsal on the day." — [Duarte: 11 ways](https://www.duarte.com/10-ways-to-prepare-for-a-ted-format-talk/)
- TEDxAlabaster: 3–4 months of scripting, revisions, rehearsals and coaching. — [Speaker Accelerator](https://speakeraccelerator.substack.com/p/tips-for-applying-to-tedxalabaster)

**Timing control**
- Duarte: practice first with the clock counting up (coach tracks time, then says how much to trim) until consistently within 18 minutes; then with the clock counting down, with benchmarks every six minutes (know the slide and line you should be on at 6, 12, 18); remove filler words; plan two ending points. — [Duarte: 11 ways](https://www.duarte.com/10-ways-to-prepare-for-a-ted-format-talk/)
- TED: "Thou Shalt Not Steal the Time of Them that Follow Thee." — [TED Commandments](https://conorneill.com/2010/06/24/the-ted-commandments-10-rules-every-speaker-needs-to-know/); the 18-minute limit is firm: "Going over would mean it's no longer a TEDx Talk." — [TEDx Speaker Guide PDF](https://tedxbend.com/tedx/manuals/tedx_speaker_guide.pdf)

**Delivery metrics**
- Eye contact: "Perhaps the most important physical act onstage is making eye contact." Pick "five or six friendly-looking people in different parts of the audience" and imagine them as "friends you haven't seen in a year." Stay still; don't shift weight; take a deep breath before walking on. — [TED Blog excerpt](https://blog.ted.com/chris-anderson-shares-his-tips-for-giving-a-killer-presentation/); [Gigazine summary](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20130613-how-to-make-killer-presentation)
- Gestures: top talks averaged 465 gestures per 18 minutes vs 272 for bottom talks; ≥240 gestures associated with high charisma; vocal variety 30.5% higher in the most popular; smiling ≥14 seconds raised perceived intelligence; the first 7 seconds predicted view counts. — [Science of People](https://www.scienceofpeople.com/secrets-of-a-successful-ted-talk/)
- Pace: recommended 120–160 wpm; presentations scoring 8+/10 had median 138 wpm; very slow (<100 wpm) usually signals under-preparation; use deliberate pauses after key points and to let a slide land; slow down for complex material, speed up for stories. — [VirtualSpeech](https://virtualspeech.com/blog/average-speaking-rate-words-per-minute)
- TED: "Use gestures to emphasize key points"; "Rehearse standing still, since pacing hurts the camera work"; avoid podiums. — [TED Organizer Guide: Rehearsals](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/rehearsals)
- Gallo: verbal delivery has four levers — rate, volume, pitch, pauses. — [Carmine Gallo](https://www.carminegallo.com/cracking-the-ted-code-takeaways-from-talk-like-ted-the-9-public-speaking-secrets-of-the-worlds-top-minds/)
- Speaker Lab on stage: "stay calm, speak slowly, use silence for emphasis, and engage the live audience first." — [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker/)
- Nick Morgan via D.M. Scott: control movement (he tended to move to burn nervous energy); "Charisma is focused emotion"; pause more; vary energy; post-event review found he "didn't pause enough and didn't vary his energy." — [David Meerman Scott](https://www.davidmeermanscott.com/blog/2013/05/how-to-prepare-and-deliver-a-tedx-talk.html)

**Research on memory and anxiety**
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Test-Enhanced Learning: Exp. 1 (120 undergrads) — restudy vs free-recall test, retention at 5 min / 2 days / 1 week: restudy 81% / 54% / 42% vs test 75% / 68% / 56%. Exp. 2 (180 undergrads): SSSS vs SSST vs STTT at 1 week: 40% / 56% / 61%; forgetting over one week ≈52% (repeated study), 28% (one test), 14% (repeated testing); repeated-study students were most confident yet retained least; benefit attributed to retrieval practice as a "desirable difficulty." — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006 (PDF)](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- Brooks (2014), "Get Excited: Reappraising Pre-Performance Anxiety as Excitement," J. Experimental Psychology: General: public-speaking experiment (140 participants) — those who said "I am excited" gave longer speeches rated more persuasive, competent and relaxed than "I am calm"; math (188) — "excited" scored 8% higher; karaoke (113) — excited ≈80% accuracy vs calm/angry/sad ≈69% vs anxious ≈53%. Explanation: anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal, so reframing is easier than calming down; saying "I'm excited" aloud can increase genuine excitement. — [APA press release on Brooks 2014](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2013/12/performance-anxiety)
- Practical nerves advice: a few days before, stop rehearsing physically and focus on mental preparation; focus on the message rather than yourself; "see yourself as the bridge between" audience and idea. — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk)
- Day-of: organizers check in, provide food and water, set aside time for miking; onstage "let a beat or two pass, take a breath, and anchor feet." — [TED Organizer Guide: Rehearsals](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/speakers-program/prepare-your-speaker/rehearsals)

### Inferences
- Combining Anderson (word-for-word memorization) with Science of People (scripted = lower charisma) and TED ("if too rehearsed, rehearse more"): the defensible standard is "memorize so thoroughly you can vary it" — i.e., over-learning past the valley of awkwardness, then deliberately rehearsing with different emphasis, audiences and improvised asides so that the recall is automatic but the delivery is live.
- A rehearsal plan the app can schedule, with each element sourced: (1) weeks 1–4: draft, then record-transcribe-edit cycles (TED); (2) first rehearsals at the "second draft" stage (TED timeline); (3) retrieval-based practice from memory rather than re-reading (Roediger & Karpicke); (4) count-up timing, then count-down with 6-minute checkpoints (Duarte); (5) at least 4–5 live audiences of varied kinds (TEDx guide; D.M. Scott); (6) bi-weekly → weekly cadence in the last two months, a two-week break, then dress rehearsals (TED); (7) on the day, one full timed run (Duarte), "I am excited" reappraisal (Brooks), breath and anchor feet (TED).
- Delivery scoring targets: pace 120–160 wpm (VirtualSpeech) with TED speakers trending ~165–175; gesture rate ≥240 per 18 min (~13+/min) as a charisma threshold; smile ≥14 s total; eye contact rotating among 5–6 anchor people; zero reading; no pacing outside the circle; pauses after key lines.

### Gaps
- No source gave a specific recommended number of full run-throughs (e.g., "20–50"); evidence is in hours (30–60+) and audiences (4–5), not counts.
- Memory-palace, "chunking by beats" and spaced-repetition schedules for speeches were not verified (search budget ran out before retrieving Cepeda et al. or speech-specific memory research).
- Filler-word rate benchmarks and a quantified eye-contact percentage were not found.
- No source described a protocol for recovering from a blank onstage; the closest is TED's "let go of the script and rely on knowing the idea" and the practice of memorizing opening/closing lines.

## Key Question 6: Marketing and amplification after the talk (publication timeline, launch prep, first-48-hours, repurposing, TED.com featuring, bios/one-sheets, paid speaking, measurement, TEDx sharing rules)

### Takeaway
TED's rules allow only link-sharing of the official TEDx YouTube upload (no re-uploads, no podcasts, no TED logo), with 60-second social clips permitted for speakers if they tag the official TEDx account; TED's own promotion guidance is to release Monday/Tuesday, write 2–3-sentence descriptions and 4–7-sentence bios, and email your network personally ("hundreds of thousands of views" have come from that alone). Publication timing is event-dependent (one month to ~two months of editing reported), fewer than 1% of TEDx talks are lifted to TED.com (about 100 per year, selected by TED's editors for "new, fresh, and relevant" ideas framed for a global audience), and the business payoff comes from using the video as a credibility asset on a speaker site/reel and pitching budget-holding conferences in your niche.

### Cited Findings

**TEDx rules on sharing the talk (verbatim where available)**
- Videos are published via the TED Media Uploader on the TEDx YouTube channel; "Talks cannot be uploaded to any other platforms or channels." No TV broadcast, streaming or audio programming except a 30-second excerpt; "No remixing or re-editing of TEDx Talks, and no podcasts based on TEDx talks." Organizers "may not share the video file with the speakers directly, instead, the link to their talk should be shared." Speakers and TEDx team members "may use up to a 60-second excerpt for 'non-commercial, social media use only'" and must tag the official TEDx account; anyone else may use up to 30 seconds. — [TEDx Rules](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/before-you-start/tedx-rules)
- Branding: speakers must state the event is "an independently organized TEDx event, not a TED event"; "Speakers must never use the TED logo"; must not suggest TED is coming to their city; in media, "Represent yourself as a participant in your specific TEDx event"; TED staff are the only official spokespeople. — [TEDx Rules](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/before-you-start/tedx-rules)
- Speaker Release grants TED "exclusive rights to distribute the recorded talk, free to the world, forever." Talks go live only after review against content and copyright guidelines. — [TED: Speaking at TEDx](https://www.ted.com/tedx/speaking-at-tedx)
- Publishing policy: TED may publish with an editor's note, limit search availability, remove, or decline to publish; reasons include inadequate documentation, uncleared copyright, later-disproved claims, or speaker-requested removal (via tedx@ted.com). No timeline is specified. — [TEDx Publishing Policy](https://www.ted.com/tedx/tedx-publishing-policy)
- Clips may be created only after the video is uploaded in the Media Uploader and published on YouTube; commercial uses (documentaries, promo videos, TV, podcasts) limited to a 30-second excerpt with no editing/mixing; clips over 30 seconds for other uses need TED distribution-team permission; organizers "aren't permitted to create podcasts using their TEDx event name, series, or content." — [TED Organizer Guide: Promote your talks](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/branding-promotions/promote-your-talks)

**Publication timeline (reported)**
- Upload approval "may take up to 3 days"; upload a few days before promoting. — [TED Organizer Guide: Get views](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/video-photography/video/get-views)
- Jia Jiang: TEDx talk in July 2015, posted to YouTube in August 2015 (≈1 month). — [Jia Jiang](https://www.rejectiontherapy.com/blog/2017/7/25/how-i-got-a-ted-talk)
- TEDxCamden: after filming, "about two months of editing and post-production." — [Reed Smith: Pariti Sutaria](https://www.reedsmith.com/de/perspectives/2023/09/tedx-insights-the-art-of-crafting-evocative-talks-with-pariti-sutaria)
- Andrew Tarvin's launch-team page: talk "launches in about a month," date pending. — [Humor That Works: Launch Team](https://www.humorthatworks.com/launch-team/)
- Peter Scott: "Not all TEDx talks go online. Published talks go through extra editing and fact-checking." — [Toastmasters: The Big Red Dot](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2024/may/the-big-red-dot)

**TED's own promotion playbook**
- Titles: be specific ("The power of song" too vague vs "A song that flies you to Machu Pichu, 1496"); use search words; short words; strong verbs, avoid forms of "be"; skip generic praise ("awesome," "great"); test "what would make you want to click." Description: 2–3 sentences, first sentence a hook (statistic, fact or question); say what kind of talk it is; for a surprise ending, "say that (but not what it is!)". Bio: 4–7 sentences answering "Why should I trust this person?" Speakers writing personal messages to their networks with a few simple steps (like, share, forward) — "Some speakers have attracted hundreds of thousands of views simply by writing to people in their networks"; email bloggers/influencers on the topic; post to fitting communities (subreddits); viewers watch at work during lunch and in the evenings, not weekends; release Monday or Tuesday; push seasonal talks when relevant. — [TED Organizer Guide: Get views](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/video-photography/video/get-views)
- Clips: "your team and speakers can create TEDx talk clips of up to 60 seconds long as Instagram Reels, TikToks, YouTube Shorts"; aim to promote each talk's core idea in 60 seconds or less; tag the official TEDx account; thumbnails 1920×1080. — [TED Organizer Guide: Promote your talks](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/branding-promotions/promote-your-talks)

**Launch and first-days tactics (speakers)**
- Launch team: members commit to be available on launch day; emailed instructions "on how you can watch, comment, and like the talk on YouTube"; rewards: digital/physical swag and an exclusive live event. — [Humor That Works: Launch Team](https://www.humorthatworks.com/launch-team/)
- Entrepreneur (TEDx speaker "Big Enough"): share more than once with a different angle each time; Instagram Story quiz with a prize; put the link in your weekly newsletter (emails with video as the primary CTA get higher click-through; "video" in the subject line can boost opens, per Vidyard's Tyler Lessard); a viewer-milestone contest (prize to the 500th viewer, repeated at 600); an email-signature line ("Watch my TEDx talk, 'Big Enough,' here."). Result: 500+ views in the first week. — [Entrepreneur: 5 Creative Ways to Get Exposure](https://www.entrepreneur.com/article/335776)
- Supporters' role after launch: watch, like, and comment on the video. — [Speaker Accelerator](https://speakeraccelerator.substack.com/p/tips-for-applying-to-tedxalabaster)
- Repurposing: turn the idea into blog posts, workshops, podcasts, articles, books, online courses; track view counts and read comments for new opportunities; record engagements as video proof for future applications. — [Patrick Frank](https://patrickfrank.beehiiv.com/p/1-how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker-a-step-by-step-guide); [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker/)

**Getting featured on TED.com**
- "The TEDx team in New York reviews every TEDx talk given during the year. A group of editors picks about 100 per year to feature on TED.com." A featured talk is published on the TED Talks Daily podcast and, three weeks after TED.com release, on TED's YouTube channel and apps. — [USN: Knut Ivar gets lifted by TED](https://usn.no/news-archive/knut-ivar-gets-lifted-by-ted)
- Jia Jiang: TED contacted the organizer about 15 months after the YouTube posting; published on TED.com Dec 6, 2016; 3M+ views; top-5 most viewed TED talk of 2017; "less than 1% of TEDx talks are featured on TED.com," so waiting is "a dumb game." — [Jia Jiang](https://www.rejectiontherapy.com/blog/2017/7/25/how-i-got-a-ted-talk)
- June Cohen (TED media): TEDx talks chosen for TED.com should be "new, fresh, and relevant"; local talks need "a sentence of context" for a wider audience; frame for a worldwide audience (a North Korea trip talk that was fine locally "sounded offensive to a global audience"). — [June Cohen transcript](https://amara.org/subtitles/0OTt05vRqUYh/en/4/download/TEDxTEDGlobal%20%20June%20Cohen%20%20What%20makes%20a%20great%20TED%20Talk.en.srt)
- Two of the most-viewed TEDx talks named by TED: Sam Berns, "My philosophy for a happy life" (TEDxMidAtlantic 2013); Colin Stokes, "The hidden meanings in kids' movies" (TEDxBeaconStreet). — [TED Organizer Guide: Get views](https://www.ted.com/participate/organize-a-local-tedx-event/tedx-organizer-guide/video-photography/video/get-views)

**Converting to paid speaking and measuring**
- "The talk is unpaid. The authority and video are the long-term assets." Feature it on your speaker website and demo video; expand into articles, books, courses, workshops; the FAQ claims "a 2 to 3x lift in paid speaking inquiries in the six months after it goes live" (unsourced). — [The Speaker Lab](https://thespeakerlab.com/blog/how-to-become-a-tedx-speaker/)
- Taylor Conroy: send the talk only after it's filmed, and only to people with budgets to pay speakers; target niche conferences; skip free/low-paid gigs; "your higher perceived value correlates to how much you get paid to speak"; company claims (unverified, marketing): a client with 5,000 views passed $75,000 in workshops/speaking; a 19-year-old reached 5M views and ~300 paid gigs; a 50,000-view guarantee; illustrative "50,000 views × 1% × $3,000 = $300,000." — [EOFire: Taylor Conroy](https://www.eofire.com/podcast/taylorconroy2/)
- Toastmasters' organizer: the Creative Commons-licensed talk "stays online for a long time, so prepare carefully." — [Toastmasters: How to Land a TED Talk](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/articles/how-to-land-a-ted-talk)

### Inferences
- A rules-compliant speaker launch kit: (1) Pre-publication: build a launch list (email list, LinkedIn connections, partners, press), prepare a 2–3-sentence description, 4–7-sentence bio, a specific verb-driven title suggestion for the organizer, and 60-second clip cut-points; do not post clips or raw footage before the YouTube publication. (2) Launch (Mon/Tue, lunchtime or evening): personal emails with 3 simple asks (watch, like/comment, forward); launch-team swarm on YouTube; LinkedIn post embedding the YouTube link. (3) First 48 hours: reply to every comment, repost with a second angle, newsletter send, signature line. (4) Weeks 1–8: 60-second clips tagged to the official TEDx account (non-commercial), quote cards and carousels built on the idea (not re-cut video), a long-form article, podcast pitches that link rather than re-use audio, and outreach to topic bloggers/subreddits. (5) Ongoing: speaker site/reel with the embed, bio line "TEDx speaker (TEDx[City] 20xx)" that never implies a TED talk or uses the TED logo, niche-conference pitches, and tracking of views, inquiries and bookings.
- Expectation-setting: the typical path to a six-figure view count is personal-network distribution plus a shareable emotional/novel idea; TED.com featuring is a <1% lottery that favors globally framed, fresh ideas and can arrive a year or more later.

### Gaps
- No official TED figure for event-to-YouTube publication time; the "4–12 weeks" range is consistent with anecdotes (≈1 month; ≈2 months post-production) but not stated by TED.
- Speaker one-sheet and bureau-specific guidance (format, fee ranges, bureau criteria) was not found before the search budget ran out.
- No independent measurement data (views-to-bookings conversion) exists beyond coach marketing claims.

### Inferences
- Weighting suggestion grounded in organizer statements: idea quality should dominate the application score (TEDxJacksonville rejects 90% on the idea alone; TED says "ideas, not profiles"; Scott: "ideas first"), so the app's application rubric should gate on the idea rubric before scoring presentation fields.
- Script and delivery rubrics should be scored at separate stages, mirroring Scott's "words right before how to say them" and the TED timeline (script lockdown before rehearsal intensity rises).

### Gaps
- No TEDx event or TED page publishes a numeric rubric or scoring levels; all level descriptors above are constructed by this researcher from the cited criteria.
- Weightings are inferred, not sourced.
